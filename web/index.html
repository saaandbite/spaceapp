Is It a Meteor Shower?

Uncovering NASA's Meteorite Trends & Impact Risks — A Data-Driven Documentary

1908
Chapter 1 · Tunguska, Siberia

The sky split open

On 30 June 1908 a space rock 50–80 m wide exploded above the Siberian taiga. It flattened about 80 million trees across 2,150 km² of forest.

It left no crater. The rock never reached the ground.

1970s
Chapter 2 · Antarctica

Scientists start looking on the ice

Dark rocks are easy to spot on Antarctic blue ice, so expeditions began collecting meteorites there by the thousands. Antarctic finds in NASA's dataset jumped from 12 in the 1960s to 4,396 in the 1970s.

Over the same decades, falls that people actually saw stayed at about 54–58 per decade.

1988
Chapter 3 · Eyes in orbit

Satellites start counting fireballs

US government sensors record bright fireballs as they explode in the atmosphere. NASA's CNEOS database holds 888 located events from 1988 to 2026, over oceans, deserts and cities.

Most incoming rocks burn up or burst in the air. Nobody collects those pieces from the sea floor.

2009
Chapter 4 · Bone, South Sulawesi

An airburst over Indonesia

On 8 October 2009 a fireball released about 33 kilotons of energy at 19.1 km above Bone, South Sulawesi. People heard the blast, and nothing hit the ground.

2013
Chapter 5 · Chelyabinsk, Russia

The flash that shook a city

An undetected asteroid about 20 m wide exploded 23.3 km above Chelyabinsk with about 441 kilotons of energy. The shockwave damaged 7,200 buildings.

More than 1,500 people were hurt, almost all by flying window glass. Many had walked to a window to look at the flash.

The question

So is Earth under a meteor shower?

The raw NASA data seems to say yes. Let's look at the clues.

↓
Before the clues

A meteor shower is not a meteorite

The title asks about meteor showers, but NASA's dataset counts something else. These are three different things.

🌠

Meteor shower

Dust left behind by a comet, mostly grains smaller than sand. They burn up about 80–120 km above us and come back on the same dates every year, like the Perseids in August.

Nothing reaches the ground

🔥

Fireball / bolide

A bigger rock that glows brighter than Venus and often explodes in the air, like Chelyabinsk. Satellites log these in NASA's CNEOS database.

Shockwave, a few fragments

🪨

Meteorite

A piece of rock or metal that survives the trip and is picked up on the ground. NASA's Meteorite Landings dataset is a list of these finds.

This is what the dataset counts
