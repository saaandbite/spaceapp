Chapter 01 · 1900 – 1969

The Quiet Sky

For most of the twentieth century, the sky behaved. People who watched it saw a meteor fall every now and then — a bright streak, a stone in a field, a story in the newspaper. The official record agreed: in the 1900s, only 136 meteorites were found in the entire decade.

And it stayed that way. Falls that people actually saw happen — Fell, in the catalog's language — held steady at 54 to 88 per full decade, from the 1900s through the 2000s. The numbers were small, and nobody thought the sky was changing.

That calm is our baseline. Every claim in this documentary is measured against it — because a mystery only exists if something actually changed. The catalog behind it: NASA Meteorite Landings, 45,716 records cleaned to 31,911.

Chapter 02 · 1970 – 1999

The Surge

Then the record exploded. Read raw, the data screams that Earth is being bombarded 66 times more often. That is the mystery this documentary investigates.

From 136 finds in the 1900s to 9,061 in the 1990s — a 66.6× jump. Scroll on, and watch the bars grow.

But the same data was hiding a lie. 6,214 records all pointed at one spot in the ocean — coordinates 0, 0, in the Gulf of Guinea. No meteorites fell there. That is Null Island: the empty coordinate a catalog writes when the real location was never recorded.

The answer is no. Earth is not being bombarded more frequently. The amber line proves it: human-witnessed falls stayed at 54–88 per full decade the whole century. The surge after 1970 is entirely caused by systematic Antarctic blue-ice expeditions — scientists actively searching for meteorites on a white backdrop, not rocks falling more often.

Chapter 03 · Antarctica

The White Desert

−60° of all clean records come from below this line

The answer was not in the sky. It was on the ice. Since the 1970s, scientific expeditions have swept Antarctica's blue ice, where dark stones stand out on a white surface and stay frozen for thousands of years. 69.25% of all clean records come from below −60° latitude.

Our K-Means analysis split the Earth into five zones. The two biggest sit on that ice — together holding 22,760 records, 71.32% of the data. One centers on −76.28°S, the other on −75.32°S.

The other clusters tell the same story from the opposite climate: the Sahara and the Arabian desert, 3,769 records — bare, dry ground where a dark stone is easy to spot.

And the rest: populated continents (3,025) and North America (2,357). This map of clusters is not a map of where meteors fall. It is a map of where humans go looking for them.

Chapter 04 · The Physics

What Falls, What Burns

Not every space rock reaches the ground. What a meteorite is made of decides whether it burns up as a fireball or survives to carve a crater — and that decides what kind of danger it is.

Ordinary Chondrite — 90.84%. Common silicate rock, 35 MPa of tensile strength. It does not reach the ground. It breaks apart high in the atmosphere as an airburst, and the shockwave does the damage.

Iron — 3.49%. Dense metal, 350 MPa. This is the one that survives entry and hits the ground whole. Rare — and the reason craters exist at all.

Achondrite — 3.09%. Crustal rock from other worlds, 65 MPa. It breaks in the mid-to-lower atmosphere — a smaller, lower airburst.

Carbonaceous Chondrite — 2.59%. The most fragile, 5 MPa. It crumbles above 35 km and almost never reaches the ground — and it carries the chemistry of the early Solar System.

Which is why Chelyabinsk — an ordinary chondrite — exploded in the air above the city instead of hitting it. NASA's CNEOS sensors recorded its flash 23 km up, releasing 441 kilotons of energy — and the shockwave shattered windows in more than 7,200 buildings. And why 1,500 people were injured by glass from shattered windows, not by a crater impact.

Interlude · 30 June 1908 · Tunguska, Siberia

The Forest That Fell Without a Crater

Accounts from the team's research archive: Space.com & Big Think (assets/articles/).

2,150 km² of Siberian forest flattened on 30 June 1908

Thirty years before the catalog even begins, the sky gave its loudest warning. On 30 June 1908, a fireball split the sky over the Siberian taiga — and 80 million trees fell across 2,150 km² of forest. An eyewitness 65 km away, S. Semenov, felt his shirt catch fire from the heat, then was hurled off his porch by the blast.

A stony body 50–80 metres across, travelling at 15–30 km per second, never reached the earth. It burst in the air at 5–10 km altitude, releasing 10–15 megatons of energy — 25 to 30 times Chelyabinsk, 600 to 1,000 times Hiroshima.

And then the mystery: when Leonid Kulik's expeditions finally reached the site in 1927, they found no crater at all. At the epicentre, trees stood stripped bare like telegraph poles — proof the blast came straight down from the sky. The most destructive meteor event in recorded history left no hole in the ground. The danger was never the impact. It was the airburst.

Chapter 05 · The Proof

The Machine Confirms

We handed the question to a machine. A Random Forest learned to guess whether a meteorite was seen falling or found later — and it was right 98.61% of the time. But the important discovery was not the accuracy. It was what the model looked at to decide.

Every bar here is one thing the model was allowed to look at: latitude, longitude, mass, and what the rock is made of. Watch which one it leans on.

Latitude alone carries 59.95% of the signal — absolute latitude 30.06% and latitude itself 29.88%. Where a rock fell matters far more than anything else.

And what the rock is made of? Almost nothing. The strongest composition signal, Iron, is 0.59%. Whether a meteorite is recorded at all is decided by where it landed and who was searching — not by a changing sky.

Finale

Not a meteor shower — a discovery bias.

Earth is not being bombarded more frequently. The surge is a human search bias, not a cosmic threat increase. The sky is steady. What changed is us: our expeditions, our sensors, our records.

But the real danger is still out there — and now you can see it for yourself.
